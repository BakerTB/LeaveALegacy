🕳️Black Holes & The Event Horizon
→A black hole forms when gravity curves spacetime so strongly that not even light has a path leading outward.
→The Schwarzschild radius defines the event horizon — the boundary of no return. If any object's Schwarzschild radius exceeds its actual radius, it's a black hole.
→Once inside the event horizon, escape would require a velocity greater than the speed of light — physically impossible.
Schwarzschild Radius
rₛ = 2GM / c²

G = gravitational constant
M = mass of the object
c = speed of light
💡Creating Light Rays
→Each light ray is represented as a particle, updated using a numerical step function over small time intervals.
→Light travels at constant speed, but its direction changes due to gravity as it nears the Schwarzschild radius — this directional change is what creates lensing.
→Previous positions are stored to create a visible trail for each ray — making the bending trajectory visible in the simulation output.
→Polar coordinates centered on the black hole simplify the distance and curvature calculations at each step.
📐Geodesics & The Schwarzschild Metric

A geodesic is the shortest path in curved spacetime — the path all objects follow when no other force acts on them. Light follows a null geodesic (massless path). We use Einstein's field equations to find this path.

→Schwarzschild's 1915 simplification: For a perfectly spherical mass in a vacuum, spacetime curvature depends only on the mass — not on surrounding matter or energy.
→Finding light acceleration: The geodesic equation gives two key derivatives per step — dr (how fast the ray moves radially toward the black hole) and dphi (how fast the angle changes).
Einstein's Geodesic Equation (simplified for light)
d²u/dφ² + u = (3/2) · rₛ · u²

u = 1/r (inverse radial distance)
φ = orbital angle
rₛ = Schwarzschild radius
📊Two Numerical Methods
Euler's Method
Uses the current slope to estimate the next position. Computes the geodesic derivatives once per frame and steps forward. Simple — but accumulates error along the curved trajectory. As noted: "Uses the current slope to estimate the next position."
Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4)
Computes the geodesic derivatives four times within each frame and averages them — giving a much better estimate of the true geodesic path. The note in the presentation: "I have failed my predecessors" — referring to how much better RK4 is than Euler for this purpose.
