🕳️Black Holes & The Event Horizon
→A black hole forms when gravity curves spacetime so strongly that not even light has a path leading outward.
→The Schwarzschild radius defines the event horizon — the boundary of no return. If any object's Schwarzschild radius exceeds its actual radius, it's a black hole.
→Once inside the event horizon, escape would require a velocity greater than the speed of light — physically impossible.
Schwarzschild Radius
rₛ = 2GM / c²

G = gravitational constant
M = mass of the object
c = speed of light
💡Creating Light Rays
→Each light ray is represented as a particle, updated using a numerical step function over small time intervals.
→Light travels at constant speed, but its direction changes due to gravity as it nears the Schwarzschild radius — this directional change is what creates lensing.
→Previous positions are stored to create a visible trail for each ray — making the bending trajectory visible in the simulation output.
→Polar coordinates centered on the black hole simplify the distance and curvature calculations at each step.
📐Geodesics & The Schwarzschild Metric

A geodesic is the shortest path in curved spacetime — the path all objects follow when no other force acts on them. Light follows a null geodesic (massless path). We use Einstein's field equations to find this path.

→Schwarzschild's 1915 simplification: For a perfectly spherical mass in a vacuum, spacetime curvature depends only on the mass — not on surrounding matter or energy.
→Finding light acceleration: The geodesic equation gives two key derivatives per step — dr (how fast the ray moves radially toward the black hole) and dphi (how fast the angle changes).
Einstein's Geodesic Equation (simplified for light)
d²u/dφ² + u = (3/2) · rₛ · u²

u = 1/r (inverse radial distance)
φ = orbital angle
rₛ = Schwarzschild radius
📊Two Numerical Methods
Euler's Method
Uses the current slope to estimate the next position. Computes the geodesic derivatives once per frame and steps forward. Simple — but accumulates error along the curved trajectory. As noted: "Uses the current slope to estimate the next position."
Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4)
Computes the geodesic derivatives four times within each frame and averages them — giving a much better estimate of the true geodesic path. The note in the presentation: "I have failed my predecessors" — referring to how much better RK4 is than Euler for this purpose.
🌀2D Kerr Lensing — Rotating Black Holes

After the Schwarzschild model, I extended the simulation to the Kerr metric — which describes a spinning black hole. Spin changes everything: the black hole now drags spacetime around it, creating an ergosphere where particles are forced to co-rotate.

→Conserved quantities: Each ray's energy E and angular momentum L are extracted from the full Kerr metric components (g_tt, g_tφ, g_φφ, g_rr) at initialization and held constant throughout integration — the way GR actually works.
→Ergosphere: Visualized at r = 2M as a yellow transparent circle. Inside the ergosphere, no ray can remain stationary — all are dragged in the direction of spin. This is distinct from the event horizon (r₊ < 2M for a₊ spinning black hole).
→Spin parameter χ = 0.9 default: Adjustable via CLI flag. At χ → 1 (extreme Kerr), the event horizon shrinks and the ergosphere grows, dramatically asymmetrizing the ray deflection pattern.
Kerr conserved quantities
E = −(g_tt · tdot + g_tφ · φdot) ← energy per unit mass
L = g_φφ · φdot + g_tφ · tdot ← angular momentum
⚡Adaptive RK45 Integrator (Dormand-Prince)

The third simulation (2DLensingKerr45.py) replaces the fixed-step RK4 with a Dormand-Prince RK45 adaptive integrator — the same algorithm used by MATLAB's ode45 and SciPy's solve_ivp. Each ray gets its own independently adapting step size.

→7-stage, 5th-order: Computes 7 derivative evaluations per step. The 4th and 5th-order solutions are compared to estimate local error — if error exceeds tolerance, the step is retried at a smaller size.
→Per-ray step size (ray[13]): Each ray stores its current step size as state. Rays near the event horizon automatically shrink their step to maintain accuracy; rays far away take large efficient steps. No global timestep needed.
→Tolerance control: Default tol = 1e-8. The PI controller clamps step scaling between 0.2× and 5× to prevent erratic jumps. Step floor at 1e-6 prevents infinite loops near singularities.
Step size update rule (PI controller)
scale = 0.9 × (tol / err)^0.2
scale = clamp(scale, 0.2, 5.0)
dl_new = max(dl × scale, 1e-6)
🌐3D Gravitational Lensing

The final simulation lifts everything into full 3D — a 20×20 grid of rays (400 total) shot through 3D space at a Schwarzschild black hole, each bending in its own orbital plane. Three integrators are available at runtime.

→Orbital plane per ray: Each ray's motion is confined to the plane defined by its initial position and velocity vectors. The simulation finds the two basis vectors (e₁, e₂) of that plane and integrates in 2D within it, then projects back to 3D for rendering.
→Flamm's Paraboloid: A cyan wireframe surface of revolution showing the actual shape of curved spacetime in the equatorial plane — the visual you see in GR textbooks. Equation: W = −2√(rₛ(r − rₛ)), plotted from rₛ to 6×10¹⁰ m.
→Three integrators selectable by CLI: Cash-Karp RK45 (default), Dormand-Prince RK45, and fixed-step RK4 — so accuracy vs. speed tradeoffs can be tested in real time.
→Interactive: Scroll to zoom, drag to rotate the 3D view. Animations can be saved as GIF with --save flag.
Flamm's paraboloid (embedded spacetime geometry)
W(r) = −2√(rₛ · (r − rₛ))    r > rₛ
X = r·cos(θ), Y = r·sin(θ), Z = W(r)
